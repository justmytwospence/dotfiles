# How my pieces are written

Calibrated on the essays I keep from individual writers. They differ in topic but share a shape.

## Opening: the point in the first two sentences

The thesis, the tension or the surprising number comes first, before any background or setup.

- An analogy that is the whole argument: "Tech companies structurally depend on writing to serve as evidence, in the same way that Bitcoin depends on proof-of-work hashing."
- A reframe: "AI is software, and we have decades of experience securing it."
- A question, then the finding with its number: "Output tokens cost about 5x input tokens. Why? I timed it: up to 29x the compute."
- A felt moment from real work: "I prompt agents and watch a wall of tool calls pile up until my eyes glaze over."
- Curiosity, said plainly: "I recently learned about an interesting way of reducing the dimension of a data set... I found it pretty."

Never open with "In this post I will", a definition, a history lesson or a setup description.

## Body: an argument, not a tour

- Each section moves the argument forward. If there are headings, they are claims in sentence form ("LLMs fundamentally break writing-as-evidence", "Prompt cards make the tacit parts explicit"), never nouns ("Setup", "Results", "Handle to DID"). Many of the best pieces have no headings at all. Under about 1,200 words, prefer none.
- Every abstract point gets a concrete instance from my own work: the exact moment, the number, the line that broke. One vivid example beats three generic ones.
- Show evidence the reader can check: a measurement, a small experiment, a chart, data, or a quote from someone who thought hard about it (Breiman, de Finetti). Code only where it is the evidence, and short.
- Name the pattern if it has no name yet, and use the name consistently.
- State the cost or the limit of my own argument ("The leverage is real, but it is not free"). Concede what is true on the other side before pushing back.
- Lists only for things that really are parallel (a handful of options, steps of a procedure), never as a substitute for paragraphs.

## Voice

First person, conversational, candid. Plain words over jargon; define a term the first time it matters. Some dry humor is welcome; hype, superlatives and "game-changer" are not. Admit uncertainty ("I don't know", "maybe in ten years I'll know better"). Short paragraphs. No marketing tone, no "Let's dive in", no emoji.

## Length

Usually 700 to 1,500 words. Go longer only when the evidence needs it, and say less rather than pad.

## Ending

End on something that turns, not a summary. One of these:

- the implication, stated sharply ("Calling an agent rogue tells us little about how a compromise happened");
- an honest open question I would like answered next ("Why is it about 10 ms per output token? I don't know yet");
- a direct, human request to the reader ("Please just write your own email. Typos and all.").

No "In conclusion", no recap of the sections, no newsletter plug.

## Outlines

An outline is the argument's beats, not a table of contents. Write:

1. **The claim**, in one sentence I would defend.
2. **The hook**: the opening scene, number or question, written out as I would actually open.
3. **The beats**: three to five steps that move a skeptical reader from the hook to the claim. For each one, say what it argues and the specific evidence or moment from the session that carries it.
4. **The concession**: the strongest objection, and where my argument stops.
5. **The ending**: the implication or open question, written out.

No section-per-topic headings, no bullet lists of facts that do not argue anything. If the beats cannot be written, the idea is not ready: say so instead of padding.
